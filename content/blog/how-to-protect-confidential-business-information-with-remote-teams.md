Bringing a remote professional into your business usually means giving them access to email, customer records, financial systems, or internal documents. That access is what makes them useful. It is also what makes security worth thinking through before day one.

The good news is that most of the protection comes from a handful of practical habits, not expensive tools. This checklist covers the essentials.

## 1. Start with least privilege

Give each person access to exactly what their role requires, and nothing more.

- A bookkeeping specialist needs your accounting system, not your HR files.
- A customer support professional needs the help desk, not admin rights to your store.
- A developer may need a development environment long before they need production.

Write down what access each role needs as part of your onboarding plan. Expanding access later is easy; recovering from unnecessary exposure is not.

## 2. Use individual accounts, never shared passwords

Every person should log in with their own account. Individual accounts let you:

- See who did what, when
- Remove one person's access without disrupting anyone else
- Enforce multi-factor authentication per person

If a system does not support multiple users, consider a password manager with shared vaults that let you grant and revoke access without revealing the password itself.

## 3. Turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere

Multi-factor authentication (MFA) is one of the most effective protections against account takeover. Require it for email, accounting, CRM, cloud storage, and anything else that holds sensitive data. Authenticator apps are generally stronger than text-message codes.

## 4. Keep data in your systems

Ask remote team members to work inside your cloud tools rather than downloading files to personal devices. In practice:

- Store documents in your shared drive, not in personal accounts
- Use your company email and chat, not personal messaging apps
- Avoid emailing sensitive spreadsheets; share links with controlled permissions

When work stays in systems you own, you keep control even when people change.

## 5. Set device expectations

You may not control a remote professional's computer directly, but you can set clear expectations:

- A supported, up-to-date operating system
- Automatic screen lock
- Full-disk encryption enabled
- Reputable antivirus kept current
- No shared use of the work device by other household members
- Secure home Wi-Fi with a strong password

For higher-sensitivity roles, consider providing a managed device or a virtual desktop so data never leaves your environment.

## 6. Put confidentiality in writing

Every remote professional should sign a confidentiality agreement before receiving access. Your service agreement should also address:

- Ownership of work product and intellectual property
- How client and customer data may be used
- Return or deletion of information at the end of the engagement
- Notification if a security incident is suspected

Have counsel review these terms, especially if you handle regulated data.

## 7. Account for regulated information

Some industries carry specific obligations:

- **Healthcare:** if a remote professional will access protected health information on behalf of a covered entity, a Business Associate Agreement and appropriate safeguards are generally required under HIPAA.
- **Legal:** law firms have professional duties of confidentiality and supervision when using non-lawyer assistance, and some jurisdictions address outsourcing directly.
- **Financial services:** customer financial information may be subject to federal and state privacy and safeguarding rules.
- **Personal data of people in other countries:** for example, the Philippines' Data Privacy Act of 2012 governs personal data processed there.

These requirements vary by situation. Identify them before work starts, not after.

## 8. Train on the threats that actually happen

Most incidents start with ordinary tricks: a phishing email, a fake invoice, or a message that appears to come from an executive asking for an urgent payment. Make sure everyone knows:

- Never change payment details based on an email alone
- Verify unusual requests through a second channel
- Report suspicious messages quickly, without fear of blame

## 9. Plan the exit before the start

Offboarding is where access often lingers. Keep a list of every system each person can access, and on their last day:

1. Disable or delete their accounts
2. Revoke shared vault and drive access
3. Transfer ownership of files and documents they created
4. Rotate any credentials they may have seen
5. Confirm return or deletion of company information

## 10. Review regularly

Every quarter, review who has access to what. People change roles, projects end, and permissions accumulate. A short, regular review keeps access aligned with reality.

## A simple starting checklist

- [ ] Access list written for each role
- [ ] Individual accounts for every system
- [ ] MFA enabled on all critical tools
- [ ] Confidentiality agreement signed
- [ ] Device expectations communicated
- [ ] Regulatory requirements identified
- [ ] Offboarding checklist ready
