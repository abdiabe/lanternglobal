Some hiring advice treats degrees as outdated, arguing that only skills matter. Other advice treats a degree as a guarantee of competence. Neither view is very useful when you are hiring a remote professional you may never meet in person.

At Lantern Global, a completed bachelor's degree is our minimum requirement for every professional we place. This article explains why we chose that standard, what we think a degree actually signals, and why it is only the beginning of a good evaluation.

## What a degree tells you

**Foundational knowledge in a field.** A bachelor's program in accountancy covers concepts like the accounting cycle, reconciliations, and financial statements. A computer science program covers data structures, algorithms, and software design. When the field of study matches the work, the candidate starts with the vocabulary and concepts the job relies on.

**Sustained commitment.** Completing a multi-year degree requires showing up, meeting deadlines, and finishing difficult work over a long period. Those habits matter in remote roles, where much of the work happens without anyone watching.

**Written and analytical practice.** Most degree programs involve research, reports, and structured problem solving. Those experiences tend to translate into clearer written communication, which is the backbone of remote collaboration.

**A consistent baseline.** When every candidate meets the same minimum standard, you spend less time filtering and more time comparing finalists on what really differentiates them.

## What a degree does not tell you

A degree does not prove that someone can do your specific job well. It does not tell you:

- Whether they have used your software
- How they handle ambiguous instructions
- How quickly they learn your processes
- How they communicate under pressure
- Whether they are a good fit for your team's way of working

A graduate who has never worked in QuickBooks Online will still need to learn it. A nursing graduate understands medical terminology but is not authorized to provide clinical care to U.S. patients. An engineering graduate can produce accurate drawings but cannot stamp them for a U.S. project. Education creates a foundation; the rest has to be tested and built.

## Matching degrees to roles

The value of a degree is highest when it aligns with the work. Some examples:

| Degree | Roles where it is especially relevant |
| --- | --- |
| BS Accountancy | Bookkeeping, accounts payable and receivable, financial reporting |
| BS Computer Science | Software development, QA, data engineering |
| BS Civil Engineering | Construction estimating, quantity surveying, project coordination |
| BS Nursing | Healthcare administrative roles such as scheduling, verification, prior authorization |
| BS Legal Management | Paralegal assistance and legal administration under attorney supervision |
| BSBA Marketing Management | Digital marketing, sales development, marketing operations |
| Bachelor of Multimedia Arts | Graphic design, video editing, motion graphics |

A related degree can also work well when paired with demonstrated skills. A psychology graduate, for instance, often brings useful perspective to recruiting and people operations.

## How we combine education with evaluation

Our recruitment framework treats the degree as the first of several checks:

1. **Educational qualifications:** a completed bachelor's degree or higher, ideally in a relevant field.
2. **Professional experience:** reviewed against the level the role requires.
3. **Technical skills:** hands-on ability with the tools and systems the role uses.
4. **Communication:** written and spoken English assessed for the role's actual needs.
5. **Role-specific assessments:** practical exercises based on real tasks.
6. **Verification and screening:** appropriate credential checks and screening, confirmed for each placement.

Then you interview the finalists. No single step decides the outcome.

## Practical tips if you are hiring directly

If you are evaluating remote candidates yourself, a few habits help:

- **Ask how the degree connects to the role.** A thoughtful answer often reveals genuine interest in the field.
- **Verify what matters to you.** If the degree is a requirement, confirm it through an appropriate method rather than relying on a resume line.
- **Never skip the practical exercise.** Education narrows the field; work samples decide it.
- **Do not overweight prestige.** Relevance of the field and demonstrated skill usually matter more than which university someone attended.

## The bottom line

A bachelor's degree is a meaningful, consistent signal, especially when it matches the job. It is not a substitute for testing real skills, checking communication, and making a careful decision. We use it as our starting standard because it raises the floor for every placement, and we pair it with practical evaluation because that is what determines fit.
