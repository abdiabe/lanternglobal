Hiring a virtual assistant in the Philippines is one of the most common first steps U.S. businesses take toward building a remote team. Done well, it frees owners and managers from hours of coordination every week. Done poorly, it becomes one more thing to manage.

This guide covers the decisions that make the difference, from defining the role to the first month on the job.

## Why businesses look to the Philippines

Several practical factors make the Philippines a frequent choice for U.S. businesses building remote support:

- **English in professional life.** English is one of the country's official languages and is widely used in higher education, government, and business, which makes everyday written and spoken collaboration practical.
- **An established outsourcing industry.** The country has a long history of providing business services to international companies, so many professionals are familiar with remote work, U.S. business norms, and common software.
- **A large university-educated workforce.** Graduates in business, accountancy, IT, nursing, engineering, and communication give employers depth across many functions.
- **Time zone flexibility.** Manila is 12 or 13 hours ahead of U.S. Eastern Time depending on daylight saving time. That allows either U.S.-aligned schedules or work that progresses while your team sleeps.

None of these factors guarantee a good hire. They simply mean the talent pool is deep enough that a careful process can find the right person.

## Decide what "virtual assistant" means for you

"Virtual assistant" covers a wide range of work. Before you look at a single candidate, decide which of these you actually need:

| Type of support | Typical work | Who it suits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive assistance | Calendar, inbox, meeting preparation, travel | Founders and executives |
| Administrative support | Data entry, scheduling, document management | Busy offices and small teams |
| Project coordination | Task tracking, status updates, follow-up | Teams juggling many moving parts |
| Specialized support | Bookkeeping, marketing, customer support, CRM administration | Businesses with a defined functional need |

If most of your needs fall into one specialized area, you may be better served by a specialist than a generalist. We explain the trade-offs in [Virtual Assistant vs. Specialized Remote Professional](/blog/virtual-assistant-vs-specialized-remote-professional).

## Write down the first 90 days

The most common reason a virtual assistant relationship disappoints is vague expectations. Write a short document that answers:

1. Which recurring tasks will they own from the start?
2. Which tools will they use, and what access will they need?
3. What decisions require your approval?
4. How will you measure whether things are going well after 30, 60, and 90 days?

This document becomes your job description, your interview guide, and your onboarding checklist.

## Choose a hiring model

There are three common ways to hire:

**Hiring a freelancer directly.** You post the role, screen applicants, and contract with the individual. This offers the most direct control and requires the most of your time. You also take on responsibility for contracts, payments, and continuity if the person leaves.

**Working with a staffing partner.** A partner recruits candidates against your requirements, evaluates them, presents finalists, and supports the working relationship. You still direct the day-to-day work. This is the model Lantern Global uses, with a bachelor's degree as our minimum requirement and a U.S. company as your contracting party.

**Using an employer of record.** If you have already found someone, an employer-of-record provider can employ them on your behalf and handle local employment administration. Recruiting and ongoing support are usually not included.

There is no universally right answer. Consider how much time you can invest in recruiting and how important continuity is to you.

## Evaluate candidates on real work

Resumes and interviews only tell part of the story. Ask finalists to complete a short exercise based on the actual job, for example:

- Reorganize a messy week of meeting requests and draft the replies
- Summarize a long email thread into five action items
- Clean up a small spreadsheet of contacts and flag duplicates

Keep it under two hours and use the same exercise for everyone. Pay attention not only to the result, but also to the questions candidates ask. Good clarifying questions are a strong signal for remote work.

Education matters too. We require a completed bachelor's degree for every professional we place, because it reflects foundational knowledge and the discipline to finish a demanding program. We explain why in [Why Bachelor's Degrees Matter When Hiring Remote Professionals](/blog/why-bachelors-degrees-matter-when-hiring-remote-professionals). A degree is a starting point, though, not proof of fit, which is why practical evaluation comes next.

## Plan the schedule realistically

Full U.S. business hours mean overnight work in the Philippines. Many professionals work those schedules successfully, but it is worth asking whether you truly need it. A few hours of overlap for meetings and quick questions, with the rest of the day spent on independent work, often works just as well and widens your candidate pool.

Whatever you choose, put it in writing. Holidays in both countries, breaks, and how schedule changes are handled should all be agreed in advance. Where a professional is an employee in the Philippines, local labor rules apply, such as those covering night work and the 13th-month pay requirement. How these apply depends on the employment structure, which is one reason many businesses prefer to work through a partner.

## Understand the full cost

When comparing options, look beyond the hourly or monthly rate. Include the time you spend recruiting, the weeks of ramp-up, software licenses, and the cost of starting over if someone leaves. A slightly higher rate with lower turnover and less management time is often the better value. We cover this in more depth in our [outsourcing guide](/resources/outsourcing-guide).

## Protect your information from day one

Give your assistant individual accounts in your systems rather than shared passwords, grant only the access each task requires, and turn on multi-factor authentication. Ask for a signed confidentiality agreement before any access is granted. Our article on [protecting confidential business information](/blog/how-to-protect-confidential-business-information-with-remote-teams) has a full checklist.

## Make the first month count

Most of the value of a virtual assistant is created by a good start:

- **Week 1:** account setup, walkthroughs of your tools, and two or three low-risk recurring tasks.
- **Week 2:** add more responsibilities, review early work together, and correct small issues quickly.
- **Weeks 3 and 4:** move toward full ownership of the tasks in your 90-day plan, with a weekly check-in.
