Hiring a remote professional is not fundamentally different from hiring someone down the hall. The same things decide success: a clear role, a fair evaluation, a thoughtful start, and honest feedback. What changes is that you have fewer chances to fix vagueness informally, so the work you do up front matters more.

This guide walks through the process we recommend to clients, whether or not you end up working with us.

## 1. Start with outcomes, not a job title

Job titles mean different things at different companies. "Executive assistant" can describe someone who only manages a calendar, or someone who runs board preparation and vendor negotiations. Before you write anything else, answer three questions:

- **What should be different in 90 days?** For example: "Invoices go out within two days of job completion" or "Every inbound lead gets a response the same business day."
- **What will this person own completely?** Ownership is more useful than a list of tasks, because it tells the professional where judgment is expected.
- **What will they never do without approval?** Payments, client commitments, public posts, and anything involving legal or medical judgment usually belong here.

Write the answers down. They become the backbone of your job description, your evaluation, and your onboarding plan.

## 2. Write a role profile

A good role profile fits on one page and covers:

| Section | What to include |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | One or two sentences on why the role exists |
| Core responsibilities | Five to eight items, phrased as ownership |
| Tools | The systems they will use daily, and which are essential versus learnable |
| Education | The degree fields that fit the work |
| Experience | The level you need, and why |
| Schedule | Required overlap with your team and time zone |
| Communication | Written, spoken, client-facing, or internal only |
| Boundaries | Decisions and activities that stay with you or a licensed professional |

Be honest about the schedule. Asking for full U.S. business hours when you only need a two-hour overlap narrows your candidate pool for no benefit.

## 3. Decide what education and experience actually matter

At Lantern Global, a completed bachelor's degree is our minimum for every professional. We think it is a meaningful signal of discipline and foundational knowledge, especially when the field of study matches the work, such as accountancy for bookkeeping or civil engineering for estimating.

A degree is not proof that someone can do your specific job. Treat it as the entry point and design the rest of your evaluation to test what the role really needs.

For experience, ask what a newer professional would struggle with. If the honest answer is "very little, once they learn our process," you may be paying for experience you do not need. If the answer is "they would make costly mistakes without supervision," prioritize experience.

## 4. Evaluate skills with real work

The single most useful evaluation step is a short practical exercise based on the job itself. Examples:

- **Bookkeeping:** categorize a sample month of transactions and explain two judgment calls.
- **Executive assistant:** untangle a conflicting week of meeting requests and draft the replies.
- **Developer:** fix a small bug in a sample repository and write a clear pull request description.
- **Estimator:** perform a takeoff on a single sheet of a plan set.
- **Customer support:** answer five realistic tickets using a short policy document.

Keep exercises short, ideally under two hours, and use the same exercise for every candidate so you can compare fairly. Score them against criteria you write before you read any answers.

## 5. Interview for judgment and communication

Use the interview to explore how candidates think, not to repeat the resume. Useful questions include:

- "Walk me through how you approached the exercise. What would you do differently with more time?"
- "Tell me about a time you noticed a mistake someone else made. What did you do?"
- "If you received two urgent requests from different people at once, how would you decide what to do first?"
- "What do you do when instructions are unclear?"

Pay attention to whether candidates ask clarifying questions. In remote work, a professional who asks good questions early saves you hours later.

## 6. Verify what matters

Decide in advance which claims you need to verify, such as degrees, certifications, or prior employment, and do it consistently. Avoid collecting sensitive personal documents until you actually need them, and store them securely when you do.

## 7. Plan the first 30 days before day one

Most remote placements that struggle do so in the first month, usually because of unclear expectations rather than lack of skill. Before your professional starts, prepare:

- Accounts with appropriate permissions in your systems
- A short written list of first responsibilities, starting with lower-risk tasks
- Examples of good work: past emails, reports, or tickets you were happy with
- A named person they can ask questions without hesitation
- A check-in schedule, more frequent at first

Our [onboarding article](/blog/how-to-onboard-an-international-remote-professional) covers this in detail.

## 8. Give feedback early and often

Small corrections in week one prevent large frustrations in month three. Give specific feedback ("this summary was too long; aim for five bullet points") rather than general impressions. Ask for feedback in return, including what is unclear or slowing them down.

## 9. Review at 30, 60, and 90 days

Use your original 90-day outcomes as the measuring stick. If things are going well, expand responsibilities deliberately. If they are not, identify whether the gap is in skills, clarity, tools, or fit, and address it directly.
